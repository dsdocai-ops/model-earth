The layers

A planet, not a picture.

Nothing on the globe is painted. Every pixel is shaded at sixty frames a second from five layers of satellite-derived data, composited the way light composes them in orbit.

8Ksurface and night-light imagery on capable displays
±0.01°solar position from an astronomical ephemeris
112atmosphere samples ray-marched for every pixel

  1. Surface

    Cloud-free true-colour mosaic of land and sea, derived from NASA’s Blue Marble programme. Oceans are deepened slightly where satellite haze flattened them.

  2. Relief & water

    A normal map computed from global elevation shades mountains at low sun. A separate water mask gives oceans a glossy surface that throws a real sun glint.

  3. Clouds

    A global cloud composite floats on its own shell twenty-nine kilometres up, drifting over the ground and casting offset shadows onto it.

  4. City lights

    NASA’s night-time imagery fades in only after civil twilight, dimmed where clouds sit overhead, exactly where the Sun has set right now.

The light

Light, the way air bends it.

The thin blue limb isn’t a glow effect. For each pixel the renderer traces a ray through a model atmosphere, sampling air density sixteen times and, at each step, marching another six samples toward the Sun.

Rayleigh scattering
Nitrogen and oxygen molecules scatter blue light about five times more than red, which tints the day-side haze and the edge of the planet.
Mie scattering
Larger aerosols scatter forward, so the rim flares bright when you orbit around to put the Sun behind the Earth.
The terminator
Sunlight grazing the day–night line crosses up to forty times more air. Blue is stripped out first, so the edge of day burns gold, then red.
